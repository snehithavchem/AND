Operator Log: Research Narrative

Adabala Venkata Snehith

Integrated PhD Student
Theoretical & Computational Chemistry
TIFR Hyderabad, India

Download CV

My research interests lie at the intersection of theoretical chemistry, computational materials science, topological quantum materials, and excited-state electronic structure theory . I am particularly interested in understanding how symmetry, spin-orbit coupling, electronic topology, and many-body interactions govern the properties of condensed-matter systems and catalytic interfaces. My broader goal is to connect first-principles calculations with the underlying physical mechanisms that determine experimentally observable phenomena.

My experience spans electronic-structure calculations in both periodic solids and molecular systems. During my master's at TIFR Hyderabad, India, I worked extensively with Density Functional Theory (DFT) and VASP to investigate electronic properties in materials, including spin-polarised systems, spin-orbit coupling, and valley-dependent phenomena. These interests have naturally led me towards the study of Berry curvature, valley Hall physics, spin-valley locking, and topological properties of electronic bands. I am particularly interested in how magnetic exchange, crystal symmetry, and relativistic effects can be exploited to engineer electronic states in low-dimensional quantum materials.

A major direction I wish to pursue is the investigation of topological and valley-dependent phenomena from first principles. This includes studying Berry curvature, anomalous and valley Hall responses, spin textures, and the role of electron-phonon interactions in intervalley scattering and carrier relaxation. I am interested in identifying materials in which symmetry breaking, magnetic order, and spin-orbit coupling provide control over electronic transport and excitation dynamics. In the longer term, I hope to explore how these properties can be connected to surface chemistry, interfacial electronic structure, and emerging catalytic applications of quantum materials.

Beyond ground-state electronic structure, I am increasingly interested in many-body perturbation theory and excited-state methodologies, particularly the GW approximation and the Bethe-Salpeter equation (GW-BSE). These methods offer a route towards understanding quasiparticle energy corrections, electron-hole interactions, excitons, and optical excitation spectra beyond conventional DFT. I aim to develop a deeper understanding of their theoretical foundations, numerical implementation, and practical application to complex materials. An important long-term objective is to connect many-body effects and optical excitations with valley-selective phenomena, spin-orbit-coupled electronic states, and light-driven processes at material interfaces.

My interests also extend to heterogeneous catalysis and organometallic chemistry. Using molecular electronic-structure methods, particularly Gaussian and ORCA, I have explored molecular electronic structure and reaction pathways in organometallic systems and metalloenzyme biomimetics. I am particularly interested in high-valent metal-oxo complexes, non-heme coordination environments, and the influence of explicit solvation on reaction energetics and mechanisms. I would like to connect these molecular-scale questions with surface reactivity, catalytic intermediates, and atomistic simulations, including molecular dynamics and QM/MM approaches where appropriate.

At the interface of materials physics and catalysis, I am interested in understanding how surface structure, charge redistribution, and electronic excitations influence catalytic activity. This includes low-dimensional systems, slab and surface calculations, metal-supported interfaces, and plasmon-assisted chemistry. I am particularly intrigued by the possibility of understanding hot-carrier generation and non-equilibrium electronic processes using density-functional embedding theory (DFET). Extending embedding approaches towards finite electronic temperatures and non-equilibrium carrier distributions is another direction I hope to explore, especially for connecting local chemical reactivity with the electronic response of an extended material.

Beyond applying established computational methods, I am interested in their numerical foundations, algorithmic design, and developer-level implementations. I hope to understand electronic- structure codes beyond their routine use, particularly the implementation and convergence of advanced methods for excited states and many-body effects. In this context, I am keen to explore the computational frameworks underlying VASP and other electronic-structure packages, with an emphasis on understanding the approximations, algorithms, and numerical challenges that govern their predictions.

Ultimately, my scientific vision is to bridge topological materials physics, many-body electronic structure, and computational catalysis. I want to understand not only what a calculation predicts, but also which physical mechanisms produce the result, how reliably those mechanisms can be described computationally, and how the predictions can be tested against experiments. I believe that meaningful progress in computational science requires close interaction among theorists, method developers, computational scientists, and experimental researchers. My guiding principle is to bring computational modelling as close as possible to experimental reality while retaining a deep understanding of the physics and chemistry underlying it.
