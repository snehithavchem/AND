Operator Log: Research Narrative

Adabala Venkata Snehith

Integrated PhD Student
Theoretical & Computational Chemistry
TIFR Hyderabad, India

Download CV

My research interests lie at the intersection of theoretical chemistry, computational materials science, topological quantum materials, excited-state physics, and computational catalysis . I am particularly interested in understanding how symmetry, spin-orbit coupling, electronic topology, and many-body interactions govern the properties of condensed-matter systems and catalytic interfaces. My broader goal is to connect first-principles calculations with the fundamental physical mechanisms underlying experimentally observable phenomena.

My experience spans electronic-structure calculations in periodic solids and molecular systems. During my master's at TIFR Hyderabad, India, I extensively employed Density Functional Theory (DFT) with VASP in solids to investigate electronic properties in materials, including spin-polarised systems, spin-orbit coupling, and valley-dependent phenomena. These experiences shaped my interest in Berry curvature, valley Hall physics, spin-valley locking, and the topological properties of electronic bands. I am particularly interested in how magnetic exchange, crystal symmetry, and relativistic effects can be exploited to engineer electronic states in low-dimensional quantum materials.

A major direction I wish to pursue is the first-principles investigation of topological and valley-dependent phenomena. This includes calculating Berry curvature, anomalous and valley Hall responses, spin textures, and understanding the role of electron-phonon interactions in intervalley scattering and carrier relaxation. I am interested in exploring how magnetic doping, inversion-symmetry breaking, and Rashba-type spin splitting can influence valley polarisation and spin-dependent transport. In the longer term, I hope to investigate connections between the electronic topology of quantum materials, interfacial electronic structure, and potential applications in catalysis.

Beyond ground-state electronic structure, I am increasingly interested in many-body perturbation theory and excited-state methodologies, particularly the GW approximation and the Bethe-Salpeter equation (GW-BSE). These approaches provide a framework for investigating quasiparticle energy corrections, electron-hole interactions, excitonic effects, and optical excitation spectra beyond conventional DFT. I aim to develop a deeper understanding of their theoretical foundations, numerical implementation, and application to complex materials. An important long-term objective is to connect many-body effects and optical excitations with valley-selective phenomena, spin-orbit-coupled electronic states, and light-driven processes at material interfaces.

My interests also extend to heterogeneous catalysis and organometallic chemistry. Using molecular electronic-structure methods, particularly Gaussian and ORCA, I have investigated molecular electronic structures and reaction pathways in organometallic systems and metalloenzyme biomimetics. I am particularly interested in high-valent metal-oxo complexes, non-heme coordination environments, and the influence of explicit solvation on reaction energetics and mechanisms. I would like to connect these molecular-scale questions with surface reactivity, catalytic intermediates, and atomistic simulations, including molecular dynamics and QM/MM approaches where appropriate.

My current research interests in heterogeneous catalysis include understanding the relationship between surface structure, charge redistribution, and catalytic reaction mechanisms. In particular, I am interested in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on metal-supported and low-dimensional interfaces, including Au/hBN/Pt heterostructures. I am intrigued by how platinum cluster structures, hydrogen adsorption configurations, metastable hydride states, and applied electrochemical conditions can influence reaction pathways and the kinetics of molecular hydrogen release. Through first-principles calculations, I aim to understand how the atomic-scale structure and electronic environment of catalytic sites influence experimentally observed activity.

Another important direction in my research vision is plasmonics and light-driven catalysis. In metallic nanostructures, light can excite localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPRs), producing collective electronic oscillations whose decay can generate energetic charge carriers. These plasmon-induced hot electrons may participate in interfacial charge transfer and drive chemical transformations that are difficult to understand using ground-state electronic-structure calculations alone. I am interested in investigating how non-equilibrium electronic excitations influence chemical reactivity, bond activation, and reaction pathways at metal interfaces.

In this context, Density Functional Embedding Theory (DFET) offers a promising framework for treating chemically active regions at a higher level of electronic-structure theory while retaining an efficient description of the surrounding material. I hope to explore embedding approaches for plasmonic systems, particularly the use of embedded time-dependent DFT and the embedded Random Phase Approximation (RPA) to investigate excited-state phenomena, charge-transfer processes, and plasmon-assisted bond activation. A further direction I wish to pursue is the extension of embedding methodologies to finite electronic temperatures and non-equilibrium carrier distributions. In particular, a dual-temperature DFET framework with distinct electronic temperatures for the active region and its metallic environment could provide a route to studying hot-carrier populations and their influence on local chemical reactions.

More broadly, I am interested in connecting many-body electronic structure, collective excitations, and computational catalysis. Understanding how quasiparticle effects, electron-hole interactions, and non-equilibrium carrier populations influence chemical processes is an exciting long-term challenge. I hope to explore how advanced excited-state methods and embedding techniques can complement conventional DFT to provide a more detailed description of light-matter interactions and surface-induced chemical transformations.

Beyond applying established computational methods, I am interested in their numerical foundations, algorithmic design, and developer-level implementations. I aim to understand electronic- structure codes beyond their routine use, particularly the approximations, algorithms, convergence strategies, and numerical challenges underlying advanced excited-state and many-body calculations. In this context, I am keen to investigate the computational frameworks behind VASP and other electronic-structure packages, with an emphasis on understanding and eventually contributing to the implementation of advanced first-principles methodologies.

Ultimately, my scientific vision is to bridge topological materials physics, many-body electronic-structure theory, plasmonics, and computational catalysis. I want to understand not only what a calculation predicts, but also which physical mechanisms produce the result, how reliably those mechanisms can be described computationally, and how the predictions can be tested against experiments. I believe that meaningful progress in computational science requires close interaction among theorists, method developers, computational scientists, and experimental researchers.

My guiding principle is to bring computational modelling as close as possible to experimental reality while retaining a deep understanding of the physics and chemistry underlying it. For me, computation is not merely a tool for obtaining numerical results, but a means of uncovering mechanisms, testing physical intuition, and developing more predictive approaches to complex chemical and materials systems.
